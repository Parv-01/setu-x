What we are solving

When services sit in different clouds, small delays and partial failures decide what users experience

Research question. In a containerized microservice application whose services are placed in heterogeneous, simulated cloud environments, how do inter-environment delay, dependency failures and client-side resilience policies (timeouts, retries, circuit breaking, fallback to a replica in another environment) affect the latency distribution and success rate? Which effects can a local simulation establish, and which need real providers?

Why it matters

  • Hybrid cloud = distinct infrastructures bound together for portability [1]; every cross-boundary call adds delay and a failure point.
  • Fan-out magnifies variability: with 100-way fan-out and 1% slow servers, 63% of requests are slow [2].
  • One mismanaged dependency raised tail latency 10.4× in a microservice benchmark [3].

Sub-questions tested

  • RQ1 Delay propagation: sequential vs parallel calls (H1).
  • RQ2 Cost and gain of timeouts, retries, breakers (H2, H5).
  • RQ3 When moving a service helps, and when not (H4).
  • RQ4 Availability recovered by cross-environment fallback, and its latency cost (H3).

Where the knowledge comes from

What the papers establish, and what they leave open

Microservice performance

Tail latency grows with fan-out [2]. Network processing is a large share of microservice time, and one bad dependency can dominate the tail [3]. Production call graphs are heavy-tailed with hotspot services [4].

Clouds vary

Public-cloud performance varies between providers and over time [5]; cloud networks make results hard to reproduce [6]. Even NetEm's realised jitter deviates from its configuration [13]. So local results are not provider measurements.

How to measure properly

Eight principles for reproducible cloud experiments, often unmet [7]; statistically sound reporting [8]. One run cannot detect a 10% regression in microservices; ten runs could [9].

Faults and resilience

Chaos engineering treats reliability as experimentation [10]. Gremlin and Filibuster inject faults to find bugs such as missing fallbacks or bad timeouts [11, 12]; circuit-breaker deployment is analysed in [16].

Placement

Placement decisions are mostly studied with models and simulators (FogTorch; YAFS app- vs service-based placement) rather than running services [14, 15].

How we used them

Directions of effects become hypotheses (H1–H5); methodology papers become built-in rules: the run is the unit of replication, raw data kept, percentiles never averaged.

Research gap

The pieces exist separately; a disciplined, runnable combination for students is missing

GapWhat the literature showsGap as scoped for SETU-X
G1Placement work is largely model- or simulator-based [14, 15]Few laptop-runnable setups measure placement effects with real containers and documented link-delay assumptions
G2Fault injection targets bug finding [11, 12]; injection tools prescribe no measurement methodA harness coupling injection with run-level replication, to quantify latency/availability trade-offs of resilience policies
G3Real cloud networks vary; emulators deviate from settings [5, 6, 13]Simulation results must carry explicit validity limits and a path to real-provider measurement
G4Cloud performance studies often miss methodology principles [7–9]A teaching platform can embed those principles by default: raw data, repeats, units, provenance

Honest scope: these are gaps in accessible, methodologically disciplined experimentation tooling for teaching and small-scale research, not a claim that no system combines these features. SETU-X is a teaching and demonstration platform with a small research component, not novel systems research.

How SETU-X helps

A working lab on one laptop, with measurement you can trust

  • Built: gateway, two inventory replicas, pricing, controller and an experiment runner in hardened containers; environment labels (aws/azure/gcp-simulated); injected link delay; bounded error/slow/hang faults; timeouts, retries, circuit breaker, fallback.
  • Measured properly: open-loop load inside the container network, every run reset and reseeded, raw rows + manifest per experiment, bootstrap intervals over runs.
  • Usable: dashboard to change placement, inject faults, run bounded experiments, watch them live, compare and export.
  • Verified on Windows: Phase 5 and Phase 6 acceptance 15/15 each; 336 automated tests, 12 browser tests. Harness check: a configured 100 ms link delay was measured as +106.8 ms on Windows (+101.7 ms on Linux), not an experiment result.
  • Helps: students and small labs can test placement and resilience choices before paying for cloud, and see which conclusions need real providers.
