# Literature Review

SETU-X · Parv Agarwal · Phase 1 · compiled 2026-10-25

This review supports the design decisions and hypotheses in `docs/research_questions.md`. It records how each source was found and verified, what was actually read, what each source supports, and what it does **not** support.

- Full evidence matrix (all required columns plus `read_depth`): [`docs/research/literature_matrix.csv`](research/literature_matrix.csv)

## 1. Method

**Search.** Started from the ten seed items in the project brief. Expanded with targeted queries on: microservice benchmarking and performance characterization; cloud performance variability and reproducibility; fault injection and resilience testing; network emulation fidelity; service placement; multi-cloud and inter-cloud brokering; distributed tracing. Tools used: web search, the Crossref and OpenAlex APIs, arXiv's API, and publisher or author pages.

**Verification.** Every DOI was resolved against Crossref by `scripts/verify_references.py`. A source counts as verified only if the registered title, first-author family name and year all match the expected values. Result: **28 of 28 DOIs verified** (the one initial mismatch was an HTML entity, `&amp;`, in a registered title, and the script now decodes it). Non-DOI sources (two arXiv preprints, one USENIX paper, one Google publication) were checked against arXiv, USENIX and Google Research pages. The NSDI author list (8 authors, pp. 513–527) was confirmed on the USENIX page because arXiv lists only six.

**Reading depth.** Recorded per source in the matrix (`read_depth`): full text, full-text summary, a specific section, or abstract only. **Claims in SETU-X documents are limited to what the recorded reading depth supports.** Detailed numerical claims are only taken from sources read at full-text or full-summary depth (Dean & Barroso, Gan et al., Meiklejohn et al., Eismann et al., the NIST definition, Burns et al., and the principles list of Papadopoulos et al.).

**Excluded.** Kratzke & Quint (2017, JSS) had verified metadata, but no abstract or full text was reachable, so it was removed rather than cited unread. Armbrust et al. (2010) is kept only for general context, because only its one-line abstract could be read.

## 2. Themes and what they establish

### 2.1 Definitions and orchestration lineage

NIST SP 800-145 [mell2011nist] defines hybrid cloud as "a composition of two or more distinct cloud infrastructures (private, community, or public) that remain unique entities, but are bound together by standardized or proprietary technology that enables data and application portability". SETU-X uses this definition. Its "local" environment plays the private-cloud role, and its `*-simulated` labels stand in for public clouds. Because these are simulations, the platform does **not** itself constitute a hybrid cloud in the NIST sense.

Burns et al. [burns2016borg] (read via the ACM Queue version of the same article) describe the move from machine-oriented to application-oriented infrastructure, with the container as the unit of management. They also describe health endpoints (`/healthz`) that let the orchestrator restart unhealthy applications automatically. This directly motivates SETU-X's per-service health and readiness endpoints, and its use of labels to attach environment placement to service instances. Borg [verma2015borg] provides the production lineage (abstract-level use only). Armbrust et al. [armbrust2010view] is general background on the potential of and obstacles to cloud computing (abstract only; no specific claim relies on it).

### 2.2 Microservice performance and failure propagation

For background on the microservice architectural style and its open problems, see Dragoni et al. [dragoni2017microservices]. Dean & Barroso [dean2013tail] show that variability in individual components is magnified at the service level by fan-out. With 100-way fan-out to servers that are slow 1% of the time, 63% of requests are slow. They also warn that naive hedging "add[s] unacceptable additional load". These are the analytical basis for H1 (critical-path propagation) and H5 (retries trade success for load).

Gan et al. [gan2019deathstar] report, on a real cluster and on EC2, that network processing is a large share of microservice execution time (36.3% for their Social Network service), and that mismanaging a single dependency raised tail latency by 10.4×. Ueda et al. [ueda2016workload] measured the microservice version of Acme Air at up to 79.2% lower throughput than the monolith on the same hardware. Luo et al. [luo2021alibaba] find heavy-tailed, tree-like call graphs with hotspot services in Alibaba's production traces.

**What these do not establish for SETU-X:** their magnitudes come from specific hardware, workloads and scale. SETU-X does not attempt to reproduce them. It reuses only the *direction* of the effects as hypotheses.

### 2.3 Cloud performance variability and reproducibility

Several measurement studies show that public-cloud performance varies:

- Between providers, and with multitenancy for some providers [leitner2016patterns].
- With daily and yearly patterns [iosup2011variability].
- In bimodal bands on EC2 [schad2010runtime].
- In the network specifically, sometimes made worse by provider QoS mechanisms [uta2020bigdata].

Corneo et al. [corneo2021clouds] measure client-to-cloud latency across 189 datacenters. Their results motivate the *order of magnitude* of SETU-X's illustrative link delays, but they cannot calibrate inter-service cross-cloud paths, which they did not measure.

**Consequence for SETU-X:** fixed injected delays on one host remove exactly the variability these studies document. Local results must not be presented as provider measurements. A real-cloud comparison needs repeated runs across time.

### 2.4 Experimental methodology

Papadopoulos et al. [papadopoulos2021methodological] propose eight principles:

1. Repeated experiments.
2. Workload and configuration coverage.
3. Experimental setup description.
4. Open access artifact.
5. Probabilistic result description.
6. Statistical evaluation.
7. Measurement units.
8. Cost.

Their systematic review found these principles often unmet in published cloud studies. Hoefler & Belli [hoefler2015scientific] give statistically sound reporting rules for performance results. Eismann et al. [eismann2020microservices] ran TeaStore on Google Kubernetes Engine (10 repetitions of 18 scenario–load combinations). They found response times unstable across runs, and single runs unreliable for detecting a 10% regression, while ten runs were reliable.

**Consequence for SETU-X:**
- The run, not the request, is the unit of replication.
- Raw observations are kept.
- Full distributions are reported with units.
- Experiment configuration and software versions are stored with every result.
- Cost appears only as an explicitly labelled model.

### 2.5 Fault injection and resilience

Chaos engineering frames reliability verification as experimentation [basiri2016chaos]. Gremlin [heorhiadi2016gremlin] manipulates inter-service messages at the network layer to test failure handling. Filibuster [meiklejohn2021filibuster] instruments HTTP client libraries to inject service-level faults during development. Among its target bug classes are misconfigured timeouts, "fallbacks to the same server" and critical services without fallbacks. Montesi & Weber [montesi2016circuit] analyse deployment strategies for circuit breakers (preprint, not peer reviewed).

Tooling also exists for delay and fault injection without modifying applications: Toxiproxy, a TCP proxy with latency, bandwidth, timeout and reset "toxics" [toxiproxy], and Istio's VirtualService delay and abort faults, which assume a Kubernetes service mesh [istiofault].

**Position of SETU-X:** like Filibuster, SETU-X injects at the client library. Unlike Filibuster, its goal is not test generation. It measures the latency and availability consequences of placement and resilience policies under controlled faults. SETU-X does not claim its injection mechanism is new.

### 2.6 Network emulation fidelity

Even kernel-level emulation deviates from its configuration. Jurgelionis et al. [jurgelionis2011netem] found that NetEm's realized jitter was lower than the configured value (delay and uncorrelated loss conformed). Becker et al. [becker2022emulation] quantify NetEm's scalability limits in large virtual testbeds.

SETU-X avoids `netem` (it also requires `NET_ADMIN`, which the project brief rules out for the normal demo) and uses application-level delay. That is a further simplification, so SETU-X (a) exports injected delay separately from measured latency, and (b) includes a validation test checking that measured latency increases by at least the injected amount.

### 2.7 Placement, multi-cloud and portability

Multi-cloud and inter-cloud surveys [petcu2013multicloud; grozev2014intercloud] describe the goals (QoS, reliability, cost) and the barriers. Opara-Martins et al. [oparamartins2016lockin] (survey of 114 participants) show lock-in concerns grow as workloads move to the cloud. Placement studies are mostly model- or simulator-based: FogTorch [brogi2017fog]; a YAFS simulation comparing app-based and service-based placement [motacruz2024placement]. Felter et al. [felter2015containers] found container overhead equal to or lower than VMs in almost all cases, but they measured one bare-metal host.

### 2.8 Observability

Dapper [sigelman2010dapper] motivates propagating request identifiers across service boundaries. Zhou et al. [zhou2021fault] report, from an industrial survey and 22 replicated fault cases, that microservice debugging practice can be improved by tracing and visualization. SETU-X therefore records per-dependency timings and routing decisions on every request. OpenTelemetry's default propagator uses the W3C Trace Context `traceparent` header [otelcontext; w3ctracecontext]. Prometheus documents that summary quantiles are not aggregatable and that averaging them is "statistically nonsensical" [promhistograms]. SETU-X applies the same rule to its own percentiles.

## 3. Research-gap derivation (evidence-based, scoped)

| # | Observation from the literature | Evidence | Gap as scoped for SETU-X |
|---|---|---|---|
| G1 | Placement work is largely model- or simulator-based, without executing real services | brogi2017fog; motacruz2024placement | Few *laptop-runnable* setups let students measure placement effects with real containers and explicit, documented link-delay assumptions |
| G2 | Fault-injection research targets bug finding (Gremlin, Filibuster); injection tools (Toxiproxy, Istio) do not prescribe a measurement methodology | heorhiadi2016gremlin; meiklejohn2021filibuster; toxiproxy; istiofault | Need for a harness that couples injection with run-level replication and reporting principles, to quantify latency/availability trade-offs of resilience policies |
| G3 | Real cloud networks vary, and emulators deviate from their settings | uta2020bigdata; leitner2016patterns; jurgelionis2011netem; becker2022emulation | Simulation results must carry explicit validity limits, and a clear path to real-provider measurement is needed |
| G4 | Cloud performance studies often fail methodology principles | papadopoulos2021methodological; hoefler2015scientific; eismann2020microservices | A teaching platform can embed these principles by default (raw data, repeats, units, provenance) |

**Scope statement.** These are gaps in *accessible, methodologically disciplined experimentation tooling for teaching and small-scale research*. They are not claims that no prior system combines these features. A broader search (for example, of chaos-engineering platforms and service-mesh testbeds) could find closer prior work. SETU-X's contribution is described accordingly in `docs/research_questions.md` §1.

## 5. Limitations of this review

- Most entries were read at abstract depth. Claims drawn from them are kept general.
- The search was targeted, not a systematic review with a registered protocol. There was no snowballing beyond the seeds, and no database-wide query counts are reported.
- Paywalled full texts (Armbrust et al.; the CACM versions of some articles) were not accessible from this environment.
- Provider documentation changes frequently. All documentation URLs carry an access date (2026-10-25) in the bibliography. One example of such a change: AWS App Runner is no longer open to new customers, and AWS recommends ECS Express Mode instead [awsapprunnerchange].
